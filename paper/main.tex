\title{Recoverability-Gated Deliberation for Release-Aware Slow Reasoning in Closed-Loop Driving}

\author{LiYi Xie}

\markboth{IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology}%
{LiYi Xie: Recoverability-Gated Deliberation for Release-Aware Slow Reasoning}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\bstctlcite{bst:etal}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models and vision--language models have demonstrated strong
capabilities in semantic scene interpretation and high-level decision making
for autonomous driving. However, their inference latency introduces a
fundamental release-time mismatch: a reasoning request is issued from one
traffic state, whereas its response becomes available only after the scene
has evolved. Existing dual-process and asynchronous driving systems focus on
when to invoke the slow reasoner or how to maintain fast control during
inference, yet they lack an explicit mechanism to evaluate whether a delayed
proposal remains valid, distinct from the current fast action, and
beneficial in the evolved traffic state. Without such a mechanism, stale
or redundant proposals may silently enter the control loop, while genuinely
corrective actions may be discarded.

This paper presents Recoverability-Gated Deliberation~(RGD), a two-stage
framework that governs both slow-reasoner admission and release validation
in closed-loop driving. At query time, RGD issues a request only when
delay feasibility, action support, recovery-cost advantage, and scene
demand are jointly satisfied. At release, the returned proposal is remapped
in the observed state and authorized only after passing feasibility,
action-distinctness, and recovery-cost checks against the current
fast command. We further introduce Recoverable Value of
Deliberation~(R-VoD), an offline matched-rollout diagnostic that quantifies
the corrective opportunity retained at release. Closed-loop experiments
on highway-env and MetaDrive demonstrate that RGD substantially reduces
slow-path invocations while preserving task outcomes under the evaluated
paired protocol, and that the release gate retains the corrective
intervention in the evaluated proposal stream while filtering harmful or
negligible alternatives.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Autonomous driving, closed-loop decision making, inference latency,
large language models, recoverability, selective computation.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{arge} language models (LLMs) and vision--language models
(VLMs) have advanced semantic scene interpretation, driving-knowledge
retrieval, and high-level decision making in autonomous driving
\cite{wen2023dilu,xu2024drivegpt4,shao2024lmdrive,sima2024drivelm,
 mao2023gptdriver,tvtVLNavigationCAV2025,tvtDrivingKnowledge2026,xin2026netroller}.
By providing long-horizon intent and contextual reasoning beyond reactive
policies, these models offer a promising deliberative pathway. In closed-loop
deployment, however, inference latency creates a release-time mismatch: a
reasoning request is issued from one traffic state, whereas its proposal
arrives in a later, potentially different state. Although a high-frequency
controller can maintain vehicle control during asynchronous inference, the
system must still decide whether the delayed proposal should influence the
current command.

For example, consider a closing lead vehicle with an initially open adjacent
lane. The system requests a lane-change recommendation, but by the time the
response arrives, the target gap may have narrowed, the maneuver may no
longer be admissible, or the fast controller may already execute the same
action. The release decision must therefore test the proposal against the
current state and the concurrent fast action.

Maintaining a fast control stream alone does not resolve this problem. A
returned action may pass an isolated feasibility check yet coincide with the
fast action or offer no cost advantage. Properly evaluating a delayed
proposal requires a common action schema, a shared mapper, and a
state-dependent evaluator that compares both branches at the release state.

Existing work addresses parts of this process. Invocation policies use
uncertainty, scene complexity, or policy discrepancy to decide when to
engage a slow branch
\cite{qian2024fasionad,zhang2025adadrive,chen2026gvlm}, and VLM-based
attention allocation prioritizes surrounding vehicles under behavioral
uncertainty \cite{tvtBehavioralAttention2026}. Asynchronous architectures
preserve fast control during model inference
\cite{chen2024asyncdriver,xin2026netroller}, while DualDrive aligns semantic
intent with a real-time policy \cite{li2026dualdrive}. Runtime-assurance
methods guard whether a candidate action may enter the actuation path
\cite{chen2022simplexdrive}. However, these approaches address invocation
timing, control continuity, or candidate safety in isolation. The critical
gap remains: to our knowledge, existing methods have not jointly provided a
release-time mechanism that remaps a delayed proposal in the evolved state,
compares it with the concurrent fast action through a shared interface, and
decides whether the proposal still offers a corrective advantage.

This paper addresses both questions within a single framework. Two
constraints guide the design: the fast controller must remain active
throughout inference, and rejected proposals must not disrupt the control
loop. RGD treats the slow reasoner as a proposal generator rather than a
replacement for the fast controller. The fast action remains the default;
a slow proposal reaches actuation only through a shared mapper and safety
interface, which also permits reasoner substitution without altering the
release logic.

A release state is recoverable when at least one feasible alternative
remains distinct from the fast action and exceeds a matched-rollout utility
margin. Actionability is evaluated at release: the returned command
must pass feasibility checks, differ from the mapped fast command, and
satisfy the recovery-cost margin. Recoverable Value of
Deliberation~(R-VoD) quantifies recoverability offline through matched
rollouts under a common continuation policy, measuring the corrective
opportunity that persists at release.

At query time, the online gate requires delay feasibility, action support,
cost advantage, and scene demand simultaneously; high urgency alone cannot
compensate for an expired maneuver window. When a response arrives, release
validation evaluates the proposal in the current state and accepts it only
if it passes feasibility, distinctness, and recovery-cost checks.

We evaluate RGD through paired allocation experiments across six
policies, controlled-delay traces, ablation studies, matched
release-state interventions, reasoner substitution, and additional
scenario-family evaluations.

The main contributions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formalize delayed deliberation through two release-time concepts,
  recoverability and actionability, and define R-VoD as an
  offline matched-rollout diagnostic of the corrective opportunity at release.
  \item We develop RGD, a two-stage framework whose conjunctive query
  admission and release gate evaluate both branches through a shared action
  interface in the observed release state.
  \item We conduct closed-loop experiments on highway-env and MetaDrive
  demonstrating that RGD substantially reduces slow-path invocations while
  preserving task outcomes, and that the release gate retains the corrective
  intervention in the evaluated proposal stream while filtering harmful or
  negligible alternatives.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Language-Conditioned and Generative Driving}

Language-conditioned driving methods differ in the proximity of their outputs to
vehicle actuation. At the advisory level, DiLu retrieves relevant experience
before producing an interpretable decision \cite{wen2023dilu}, and Driving with
LLMs represents traffic through object-level vectors and returns actions with
explanations \cite{chen2024drivingllms}. VLM-based systems combine visual
observations with language reasoning for navigation and motion planning
\cite{tvtVLNavigationCAV2025,tvtVLMDriver2026}, while knowledge retrieval methods
supply domain-specific context before decision making
\cite{tvtDrivingKnowledge2026}. These systems use language-conditioned
interfaces to encode scene relations and retrieve driving knowledge.

A second group places model outputs closer to the control boundary. Policy
transfer methods use language models to support vehicle decision making
\cite{titsPolicyTransfer2025}, GPT-Driver casts planning as language modeling
\cite{mao2023gptdriver}, and C-TRAIL integrates commonsense reasoning with
trajectory planning \cite{tvtCtrail2026}. Generative driving methods learn
predictive scene representations or future trajectories for downstream planning
\cite{wang2023drivedreamer,min2024driveworld,zheng2024genad,
liao2025diffusiondrive}. As outputs approach actuation, a delayed proposal may
have fewer downstream opportunities for correction and should be evaluated in
the state in which it will execute.

These studies focus primarily on model capability or driving representation.
By contrast, RGD operates at the interface between a slow proposal generator
and the fast controller, evaluating whether a delayed proposal should replace
the current fast action in the state where it will actually execute.

\subsection{Dual Process Reasoning and Selective Computation}

Dual-process driving systems divide responsibility between a responsive policy
and a deliberative model. LeapAD uses reflection and accumulated experience,
LeapVAD adds cognitive perception, and FASIONAD coordinates both branches
through adaptive feedback \cite{mei2024leapad,ma2026leapvad,qian2024fasionad}.
DualDrive aligns semantic intent from a VLM with a real-time policy without
relying on buffering or asynchronous updates \cite{li2026dualdrive}, and
NetRoller connects general and specialized models through asynchronous
execution with explicit handling of differing model frequencies
\cite{xin2026netroller}. Both designs keep a fast branch active while the
deliberative component contributes at a system-defined rate.

Selective computation methods determine when the slow branch should run.
AdaDrive adapts deliberation depth to scene demand
\cite{zhang2025adadrive}, and AdaThinkDrive selects between fast direct
answers and slow chain-of-thought reasoning modes according to scene
complexity \cite{luo2025adathinkdrive}. G-VLM uses behavior discrepancy
between lightweight and multimodal policies as a self-supervised training
signal for an online invocation gate \cite{chen2026gvlm}. Behavioral
Attention allocates VLM attention among surrounding vehicles under
behavioral uncertainty \cite{tvtBehavioralAttention2026}, addressing
perceptual resource management rather than slow-path invocation timing.
These signals are evaluated before inference begins, when neither the
returned action nor the release state is known. By contrast, RGD
supplements query-time selection with an explicit release-time evaluation
against the concurrent fast action, ensuring that a successfully issued
request does not automatically receive release authority.

Metareasoning balances expected decision improvement against computational cost.
Time-bounded and anytime methods schedule reasoning under a finite budget
\cite{russell1991metareasoning,boddy1994deliberation,zilberstein1996anytime,
cserna2017planning}, and concurrent planning permits an executor to continue
acting while a planner computes \cite{elboher2023concurrent}. In driving, an
estimate made at query time can differ from the opportunity available at
release because traffic evolves while the fast controller continues to act.
R-VoD measures the remaining corrective opportunity through matched
rollouts.

\subsection{Runtime Safety, Delay, and Viability}

Timing mechanisms preserve control during inference. AsyncDriver separates
language-model planning from real-time control
\cite{chen2024asyncdriver}, NetRoller keeps a specialized driving model active
\cite{xin2026netroller}, event-triggered control adjusts the timing of
stabilizing control updates \cite{tabuada2007event}, and edge-computing
frameworks schedule offloaded computation across heterogeneous resources
\cite{li2021edgecomputing}.
Delay-aware control further models state evolution during input lag
\cite{molnar2023delay}. At the assurance layer, Simplex-based systems revert to
a trusted controller when a learned action fails an acceptance test
\cite{chen2022simplexdrive}, and formal safety and viability methods
characterize admissible motion under dynamic constraints: RSS defines
responsibility-sensitive safety distances \cite{shalevshwartz2017rss}, and
viability theory provides invariance certificates for continuous dynamics
\cite{liniger2019viability}.

These approaches address timing, invocation, or candidate safety individually
but do not compare the delayed proposal with the concurrent fast action as
a distinct release-time decision. RGD complements runtime safety by adding
an explicit release-time evaluation: beyond checking whether a mapped action
can enter actuation, the release gate tests whether the proposal is distinct
from, and offers a recovery-cost advantage over, the fast action in the
evolved state. The final safety projection remains active after selection.

\section{Recoverability-Gated Deliberation}
\label{sec:method}

RGD decomposes slow-reasoner invocation into two sequential authority decisions:
query admission and release validation. Admission determines whether the current
scene warrants deliberation and whether a feasible alternative can be supported
within the expected response interval. Release validation evaluates the returned
proposal against the observed state at the time of delivery. The fast controller
remains the default throughout inference and is retained whenever either stage
withholds slow-path authority.

A release state $s_r$ is \emph{recoverable} if it admits at least one feasible
effective alternative that is distinct from the fast effective action and exceeds
the R-VoD margin. R-VoD quantifies this corrective opportunity offline via
matched rollouts. A returned proposal is \emph{actionable} at release only if
its mapped command passes current-state feasibility, differs from the mapped fast
command, and satisfies the proposal-specific recovery-cost margin. The final
safety projection may subsequently collapse an authorized slow command to the
same executable action as the fast command; R-VoD and release validation thus
serve complementary, not interchangeable, roles.

Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} illustrates the two online gates and the offline R-VoD
diagnostic branch. The fast controller remains active at every control cycle; a
missing response, mapping failure, or rejected release leaves the fast command
unchanged. Both branch outputs are projected into the same mapped command space
prior to the shared feasibility and cost interfaces, ensuring that semantically
equivalent expressions resolve to the same maneuver irrespective of reasoner
phrasing.

\subsection{Query and Release Process}

At frame $t$, the ego vehicle observes state $s_t$. The fast policy produces an
action that the shared mapper converts to $a_t^f$. Both branches share this
mapper and the same final safety projection $\Phi(s,a)$, which maps a command
to an executable action in state $s$. Branch outputs are parsed into a maneuver
and a target speed; two expressions are considered equivalent if they induce the
same mapped behavior. The high-level action domain comprises \textit{lane-left},
\textit{lane-hold}, \textit{lane-right}, \textit{accelerate}, and
\textit{decelerate}, all resolved through the same simulator interface.
The response parser $\psi(y)$ normalizes a structured action identifier or
canonical token; $\mathcal M(s,y)=\psi(y)$ if and only if
$\psi(y)\in\mathcal A(s)$, and is undefined otherwise. This constitutes
current-state semantic mapping rather than trajectory replanning.

The returned proposal is remapped in the observed release state $s_r$. A
lane-change or speed command that was feasible at query time may no longer be
valid; any mapping or feasibility failure causes $a_r^f$ to be retained.

The delay estimate is used solely at admission. The actual release frame $r$ is
the first control frame at which the response becomes available, and both the
returned command and the fast command are evaluated in the observed state $s_r$.
Responses that do not arrive before episode termination are discarded.

\subsection{Offline Measure of Corrective Opportunity}

Offline matched rollouts provide a state-level diagnostic of the corrective
opportunity available at release, independent of any specific slow-reasoner
response. At each logged release snapshot $s$, let
$a_{\mathrm{eff}}^f=\Phi(s,a^f)$ denote the effective fast first action under
the shared mapper and final safety projection. Let $\mathcal D(s)$ be the set
of feasible effective first actions that remain distinct from
$a_{\mathrm{eff}}^f$ after the same projection stages, and let
$\mathcal C(s)=\{a_{\mathrm{eff}}^f\}\cup\mathcal D(s)$. The matched-rollout
utility $J_H(s,a)$ for horizon $H\in\mathbb N_+$ and discount $\gamma\in[0,1]$
is defined as
\begin{equation}
J_H(s,a)=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^k r_k}
{\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^k}
-\mathbb{I}[E_C],
\label{eq:rollout_utility}
\end{equation}
where $s_0=s$, $a_0=a$, the fast policy supplies $a_k$ for $k\ge1$,
$r_k$ is the per-step environment reward, and $E_C$ is the event that a
collision occurs within the rollout horizon; a collision terminates the rollout
and incurs a unit penalty. We set $H=20$ and $\gamma=0.99$; candidates sharing
the same projected command and target speed are merged prior to evaluation. The
Recoverable Value of Deliberation (R-VoD) is then defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{R\text{-}VoD}(s)=
\max_{a\in\mathcal C(s)}
\left[J_H(s,a)-J_H(s,a_{\mathrm{eff}}^f)\right].
\label{eq:rvod}
\end{equation}
Since $a_{\mathrm{eff}}^f\in\mathcal C(s)$, R-VoD is nonnegative by
construction and equals zero when no alternative improves upon the fast
continuation. A state is labeled \emph{corrective} when
$\mathrm{R\text{-}VoD}(s)\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{R}}$, where
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{R}}=0.02$ is a fixed utility-scale margin.

By varying only the first effective action while holding the remainder of the
trajectory under the common fast policy, R-VoD isolates the corrective
opportunity at release from any downstream dependence on the slow-reasoner
response.

\subsection{Query Admission}

Let $\mathcal A(s_t)$ denote the exact runtime action domain and $a_t^f$ the
mapped fast command. The runtime recovery-cost evaluator is
\begin{equation}
c_t(a)=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
h_t+\left[d_t(a)-\min_{b\in\mathcal A(s_t)}d_t(b)\right]_+\right),
\label{eq:recovery_cost}
\end{equation}
where $[x]_+=\max(x,0)$; $d_t(a)\in[0,1]$ is the fixed-weight penalty
combining safety, comfort, and efficiency costs; and $h_t\in[0,1]$ is the
scene-level hazard aggregating TTC urgency, headway deficit, obstacle
proximity, and interaction pressure. Lower values of $c_t$ are preferred.
Malformed cost inputs or inconsistent action domains suppress admission. The
domain integrity guard $F_t$ requires consistent action-domain agreement and
a nonempty feasible-alternative set
$\mathcal D_t=\{a\in\mathcal A(s_t)\setminus\{a_t^f\}:c_t(a)\leq\kappa_c\}$,
where $\kappa_c>0$ is the recovery-cost feasibility threshold; $F_t=0$ and no
slow request is issued whenever $\mathcal D_t=\varnothing$.

Delay feasibility is characterized by control frequency $f$, predicted latency
$\widehat\tau_t$, critical maneuver window $T_t^{\mathrm{crit}}$ (estimated
from the current TTC and admissible trajectory corridor), and safety reserve
$\rho$. The residual-window score is
\begin{equation}
\ell_t=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
\frac{T_t^{\mathrm{crit}}-\lceil f\widehat\tau_t\rceil/f-\rho}
{T_t^{\mathrm{crit}}}\right).
\label{eq:residual_window}
\end{equation}
Unavailable timing evidence sets $L_t=0$.
Let $\mathcal F$ denote the fixed set of non-Fast maneuver families,
$\mathcal F_t\subseteq\mathcal F$ those represented in $\mathcal D_t$,
$u_m$ the minimum recovery cost among members of family $m$ in $\mathcal D_t$,
and $u_*=\min_{m\in\mathcal F_t}u_m$. Normalizing by $|\mathcal F|$ scores
family coverage as an absolute fraction of all families, so that absent
families penalize the score; $\mathcal F_t=\varnothing$ sets $A_t=0$.
The four admission tests are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:admission_tests}
\begin{align}
L_t&=\mathbb{I}[\ell_t\geq\lambda_L],
\label{eq:test_L}\\
A_t&=\mathbb{I}\!\left[|\mathcal F|^{-1}\!\sum_{m\in\mathcal F_t}
e^{-(u_m-u_*)/T_A}\geq\lambda_A\right],
\label{eq:test_A}\\
H_t&=\mathbb{I}\!\left[\kappa_c^{-1}
[c_t(a_t^f)-\min_{a\in\mathcal D_t}c_t(a)]_+\geq\lambda_H\right],
\label{eq:test_H}\\
N_t&=\mathbb{I}[\max(h_t,n_t^{\mathrm{pre}})\geq\lambda_N],
\label{eq:test_N}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $n_t^{\mathrm{pre}}\in[0,1]$ is an independent scene-hazard pre-screen
score derived from obstacle density and conflict geometry, and $T_A>0$ is the
soft-aggregation temperature for family support. All thresholds
$(\lambda_L,\lambda_A,\lambda_H,\lambda_N)$ are selected on the development
cohort and frozen prior to the reported evaluation; all four conditions must
hold simultaneously.

Let $v_t\in\{0,1\}$ encode resource availability, including request budget,
cooldown, and executor status, subject to the requirement that no slow request
is already pending. The admission rule is
\begin{equation}
q_t=\mathbb{I}\!\left[v_t\wedge F_t\wedge L_t\wedge A_t\wedge H_t\wedge N_t\right],
\label{eq:query_admission}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function.

\subsection{Release Validation}

At release frame $r$, let $y_r$ denote the returned response and $\mathcal M$
the shared current-state mapper. The fast policy independently produces $a_r^f$
from the same state $s_r$. If $\mathcal M(s_r,y_r)$ is undefined, the response
is rejected and $a_r^f$ is retained. Otherwise, let
$\widetilde a_r^{\mathrm{sl}}=\mathcal M(s_r,y_r)$ denote the mapped slow
command. The state-level predicates $F_r$, $A_r$, $H_r$ and the cost function
$c_r$ are recomputed in $s_r$; delay feasibility and scene demand are
admission-only conditions that do not re-enter release. The feasible slow-command
set is defined as
$\mathcal V_r=\{a\in\mathcal A(s_r):c_r(a)\leq\kappa_c\}$ if
$F_r\wedge A_r\wedge H_r$ holds, and $\mathcal V_r=\varnothing$ otherwise.
The release indicator is
\begin{equation}
g_r=\mathbb{I}\!\left[
\widetilde a_r^{\mathrm{sl}}\in\mathcal V_r
\wedge\widetilde a_r^{\mathrm{sl}}\neq a_r^f
\wedge c_r(\widetilde a_r^{\mathrm{sl}})+\delta\leq c_r(a_r^f)
\right],
\label{eq:release_stages}
\end{equation}
where $\delta\geq0$ is the release margin (expressed on the same scale as
$c_r$), fixed at $\delta=0.02$.

The selected and executed commands are accordingly
\begin{equation}
a_r^{\mathrm{sel}}=
\begin{cases}
\widetilde a_r^{\mathrm{sl}}, & g_r=1,\\
a_r^f, & g_r=0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
a_r^{\mathrm{exec}}=\Phi(s_r,a_r^{\mathrm{sel}}).
\label{eq:execution_contract}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:execution_contract} makes the fast-path fallback explicit:
whenever $g_r=0$, $a_r^{\mathrm{exec}}=\Phi(s_r,a_r^f)$. Distinctness is
evaluated between mapped commands prior to the projection $\Phi$; consequently,
an authorized slow command may still map to the same executable action as the
fast command after projection, and this post-projection outcome is reported
separately. Query admission confers no persistent authority on a returned
proposal, and no R-VoD label enters the online release decision. All checks
operate on the observed state $s_r$ and authorize only proposals that provide
a strict recovery-cost advantage over the concurrent fast action.

\section{Experiments and Discussion}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

RGD is evaluated on highway-env \cite{lesort2018highwayenv} and MetaDrive
\cite{li2023metadrive} closed-loop simulators with Qwen3-8B
\cite{qwen3technicalreport} as the default slow reasoner. The main highway-env
experiments run at 10~Hz for 30~s per episode. Within each comparison, all
methods share the seed cohort, action schema, fast controller, slow-reasoner
interface, request budget, and evaluation rule. Gate development, main
evaluation, and mechanism analysis use disjoint cohorts. The paired allocation
study uses 30 seeds and six policies, yielding 180 policy--seed units. Each
component study draws from a separate 20-seed cohort; the six-condition
ablation study contains 120 paired condition--seed units.

The simulator seed serves as the experimental and bootstrap unit. We report
95\% percentile bootstrap intervals from 20,000 resamples. Binary paired
endpoints use exact McNemar tests, continuous endpoints use paired sign-flip
tests, and the five prespecified RGD--baseline contrasts are Holm-adjusted
within each endpoint. R-VoD is computed after release through matched
first-action rollouts. Gate criteria, corrective margins, and the
service-latency stratum are selected on the development or calibration
cohorts and remain frozen throughout the reported evaluation.

The analyses span six properties: published-settings context, admission
selectivity under delay sweep, release-gate validation, gate criterion
ablations, reasoner substitution, and cross-scenario transfer. The gate
thresholds are selected on a held-out development cohort (seeds 2000--2039)
across three delay strata (0.7, 1.7, 2.7~s), maximizing the corrective-state
margin while minimizing requests per corrective state.

The paired allocation cohort provides the primary resource and task-endpoint
comparison. Separate controlled-delay cohorts support event-level analysis of
timing, validation, and projection. To isolate release validation, a matched
audit freezes the 11 proposals naturally issued by RGD on the 30 paired seeds;
the release-validated and no-validation arms receive the same proposals and
release timing. Every post-projection distinct action is compared with Fast
from its matched online release snapshot using the matched-rollout
utility~\eqref{eq:rollout_utility}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:simulator_scenes} shows representative highway-env and
MetaDrive scenes. The upper row covers highway, merge, roundabout, and
intersection geometries; the lower row shows the corresponding MetaDrive
reconstructions.

\subsection{Delay-Controlled Allocation and Component Evidence}

\subsubsection{Paired Common-Protocol Allocation}

The paired allocation study compares RGD against five scheduling baselines
under a common 30-seed protocol: Fast-only (never invokes the slow reasoner),
Always Slow (issues a request at every eligible frame), Random (requests with
uniform probability at each eligible frame), Uncertainty (requests when the
fast policy entropy exceeds a threshold), and Risk (requests when the current
TTC-urgency score $h_t$ exceeds a threshold). All methods share the same
Qwen3-8B service, fast controller, action mapper, release interface, and
episode realization; only the slow-path allocation policy differs.
Fig.~\ref{fig:formal_allocation}(a) reports requests per episode, and
panel~(b) reports the paired RGD-minus-baseline effects for the two
request-intensive allocators.

RGD averages 0.37 requests per episode, reducing slow-path use by 90.5\%
relative to Always Slow and by 84.5\% relative to Random. The paired
differences are $-3.50$ requests [95\% CI $-4.00$, $-3.00$] and $-2.00$
requests [95\% CI $-2.47$, $-1.53$], with standardized effects $d_z=-2.49$
and $-1.49$, respectively. Both contrasts remain significant after Holm
correction ($p_{\mathrm{H}}=0.00025$). Fast-only and Uncertainty provide
zero-request references without engaging the slow reasoner.

\subsubsection{Zero Added Delay Reference}

The zero-delay cohort holds the fast controller, slow reasoner, request
budget, and feasibility interface fixed across all six allocation policies;
only the request and release rules differ. The delay sweep then uses the
same seeds and opportunity schedule to assess delay-feasibility effects on
slow-path allocation.

Without added delay, Fig.~\ref{fig:zero_delay_extension}(a)--(b) shows that
RGD schedules fewer requests and achieves lower per-frame runtime than Always
Slow. Across the delay sweep in Fig.~\ref{fig:zero_delay_extension}(c),
request counts remain similar at 0 and 0.7~s and decrease at 1.7 and 2.7~s while distance intervals overlap,
consistent with the delay-feasibility condition inducing greater selectivity
without per-delay retuning.

\subsubsection{Allocation With a 1.7-s Added Delay}

The 1.7-s experiment examines how RGD processes a slow response after the
traffic state has evolved. The fast controller, slow reasoner, request budget,
seed cohort, and feasibility interface remain fixed.
Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_profile} reports the response-validation stages and
the resulting release decisions.

Each returned proposal arrives in a state later than the state that initiated
the request. RGD applies a returned proposal only after all release checks
pass.

\subsubsection{Response Validation and Component Effects}

Response traces follow each request from admission through response
validation, current-state mapping, and actuation. At the same 1.7-s added
delay, the one-at-a-time ablation variants draw from a separate 20-seed
cohort; each removes one admission criterion while retaining the release
procedure.

Under 1.7-s delay, 143 RGD requests return valid responses and 138 reach
release before episode termination. Current-state checks admit 28 responses
to release comparison; nine mapped slow commands differ from the concurrent
fast command, and six satisfy the recovery-cost margin. After the shared
final projection $\Phi$, four of these authorized commands match the
projected fast command and two remain distinct.
Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_profile}(a) reports the validation stages from valid response return;
panel~(b) summarizes all 28 state-valid outcomes. A delayed proposal affects
the final command only after passing all release checks and remaining distinct
after projection; otherwise the fast action stays in control.

Fig.~\ref{fig:components}(a)--(b) shows that removing $L$, $A$, or $H$
changes both the number of requests and the additional release states
relative to Full RGD\@. Panel~(c) reports the ablation-minus-Full-RGD
difference in R-VoD-labeled corrective-state rate with 95\% seed-bootstrap
confidence intervals. The $H$ contrast has a confidence interval entirely
above zero, directly linking the online cost-advantage condition to the
corrective-state rate of the selected cohort. The request and
additional-release-state changes in panels~(a)--(b) further show that $L$
and $A$ shape which states are sent to the slow path. Together, these
analyses confirm the complementary roles of delay feasibility, action
support, and recovery-cost advantage in the admission rule.

The corrective-state rate in Fig.~\ref{fig:components}(c) is labeled offline
by R-VoD, whereas $H_t$ is computed online. The $H$ contrast thus links an
online admission signal to a separately constructed release-state
diagnostic.

\subsubsection{Full-Gate and Projection Analysis}

Whereas the preceding ablation tests admission-criterion effects under
natural request flow, this analysis replays a shared proposal bank to
isolate gate and projection factors independently of scheduling variation.
Six conditions are evaluated: Full RGD, removal of each of $L$, $A$, $H$,
and $N$, and joint removal of $H$ and $N$. All conditions replay the same
gate-independent proposal source over 20 held-out seeds at a fixed 1.7-s
delay. The action mapper, release rule, safety projection, budget, and
cooldown remain unchanged, so each comparison removes only the named
admission predicate.

The shared bank is frozen from a Qwen3-8B Always-Slow source that does not
read the evaluated gate. Each record binds the source frame, raw action,
response outcome, measured latency, and response hash; all arms replay the
same bank and recompute the Fast action at every frame. Gate decisions are
completed before proposal fields are exposed to an arm.

Fig.~\ref{fig:formal_components}(a) traces the 70 synchronized candidates
through the serial admission stages. Full RGD retains 49 candidates after
$L$, 40 after $A$, eight after $H$, and none after $N$. Removing $L$
retains 70 candidates at the first stage and 61 after $A$; removing $A$
retains 49 after the second stage. These trajectories expose the distinct
upstream screening roles of $L$ and $A$. Removing $H$, $N$, and $H/N$
leaves 1, 8, and 40 candidates after the complete gate, respectively.
Panel~(b) follows these three conditions through the slow-path processing
stages: 1, 8, and 40 requests are issued, of which 1, 5, and 26 reach
delayed release. This analysis separates candidate reduction by $L/A$ from
the final workload control provided by $H/N$.

The command-level trace separates release exposure from executable effect.
Removing $N$ and $H/N$ yields 4 and 16 mapped proposals distinct from the
fast command. The shared final projection maps all of them to the
fast-equivalent executable action, and task endpoints remain invariant
across all 120 paired condition--seed units. This ablation identifies the
dominant $H\times N$ interaction in slow-path workload and confirms that
the fast-path fallback is preserved at the execution boundary.

The matched-proposal release analysis replays the 11 natural RGD proposals
through the current release gate. Both release-validated and no-validation
conditions obtain nine releases. Without validation, three returned commands
remain distinct after the common projection; release validation retains one.
Fig.~\ref{fig:formal_components}(c) reports their matched utility. The
retained action improves the utility~\eqref{eq:rollout_utility} from 0.667
to 0.721, an 8.2\% relative gain, and increases 20-step progress by
23.70~m without a collision in either branch. The two filtered actions have
utility advantages of $-0.0325$ and $0.0047$, both below the fixed 0.02
corrective margin. The release gate thus preserves the only corrective
intervention in the fixed proposal stream and removes the harmful and
negligible alternatives.

The slow reasoner is replaced with six alternative models while the RGD

Table~\ref{tab:multi_llm} shows that distance and completion remain tightly
aligned across all seven models: valid responses range from 112 to 138 per

\subsection{Evaluation Across Lane and Density Conditions}
\label{subsec:traffic}

Table~\ref{tab:traffic_stress} reports mean distance and successful runs
across six lane/density configurations without retuning the gate. At low
density (2.0), successful runs reach 28--30 and mean distance 599--629~m.
At high density (3.0), successful runs drop to 20--23 and mean distance
decreases to 429--478~m, consistent with the increased task difficulty under
dense traffic. RGD operates across the evaluated lane counts and vehicle
densities with no per-setting threshold adjustment.

\subsection{Cross-Scenario Evaluation}

\subsubsection{Maneuver Classes and Traffic Density}

Following the five maneuver classes, three left-turn density levels, and
1,000 episodes per cell reported by Ma~et~al.~\cite{tvtBehavioralAttention2026},
RGD is evaluated at 2~Hz for 30~s. Table~\ref{tab:bua_transfer} reports the
resulting success rates. RGD achieves 0.986 on merge scenarios and maintains
above 0.80 success across all three density levels. On roundabout and
left-turn scenarios, success rates are lower than those of the
VLM attention-allocation baseline, which applies scenario-specific
uncertainty-weighted attention trained on those scene types. RGD uses a
single scenario-agnostic release gate; the gap on complex maneuvers is
consistent with the absence of scenario-specific fine-tuning.

\subsubsection{Compound Hazard Scenes}

Following the scenario and metric definitions of Wang~et~al.~\cite{titsPolicyTransfer2025},
Table~\ref{tab:policy_transfer} reports collision-free safety rates and mean
speed in three MetaDrive compound-hazard scenes. RGD attains 100\%
collision-free safety in Scene~A (pedestrian crossing) and 94.8\% in
Scene~B (uncontrolled intersection), with a single gate configuration and
no scenario-specific policy training. In Scene~C (combined
vehicle--pedestrian traffic), RGD records 84.6\% safety. The lower rate
relative to ASR-RL is consistent with the advantage of trained
scenario-specific policies in high-complexity long-tail environments; RGD
uses no scenario-specific policy training or gate retuning in this evaluation.

\subsection{Discussion}

RGD converts slow reasoning from a persistent command source into a
release-conditioned proposal, decoupling the decision to invoke deliberation
from the decision to act on its output. The admission gate restricts
requests to states where a feasible corrective alternative exists within the
delay window; the release gate restricts actuation to proposals that offer a
demonstrable advantage over the concurrent fast action in the evolved state.

The paired common-protocol study establishes the principal result: RGD
reduces slow-path requests by over 90\% relative to continual invocation
while preserving per-seed task outcomes, occupying a selective operating
point between the overhead of always-slow reasoning and the inflexibility of
always-fast control. Component ablations show that $H$ and $N$ jointly
govern release exposure while $L$ and $A$ determine which states reach the
slow path, with each admission criterion playing a distinct and non-redundant
role. The matched replay confirms that release validation retains the
corrective proposal and suppresses the harmful and negligible alternatives.

Reasoner substitution shows that system-level performance remains stable
across the evaluated reasoners, indicating that system-level outcomes are
determined primarily by RGD's internal gating, shared action mapping, and
fallback mechanisms rather than by standalone model performance. Across the full
range of lane counts and vehicle densities, RGD operates without threshold
retuning, with the resulting mean distance and successful-run counts reported
in Table~\ref{tab:traffic_stress}. The MetaDrive
results further support consistent gate operation across compound-hazard scenes
outside the primary highway setting.

\noindent\textit{Limitations and future work.}
The current evaluation uses discrete high-level commands and controlled,
measurable response delays. In long-tail scenarios such as dense
roundabouts or mixed vehicle--pedestrian traffic, the absence of
scenario-specific fine-tuning limits performance relative to methods with
targeted policy libraries. RGD also depends on the slow reasoner producing
structured output parseable by the shared mapper; models not
instruction-tuned to the action schema may yield lower response validity.
Future work will target fine-tuned driving models for rare maneuver
coverage, continuous trajectory proposals with delay-aware replanning, and
real-world validation beyond simulator dynamics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents Recoverability-Gated Deliberation, a two-stage framework
that governs when to invoke slow reasoning and whether a delayed LLM proposal
may enter the closed-loop control path. By formalizing query admission and
release validation as separate authority decisions, RGD ensures that a delayed
proposal reaches actuation only when it is feasible, distinct from the
current fast action, and offers a recovery-cost advantage in the evolved
traffic state. The companion R-VoD diagnostic provides an offline
measure of the corrective opportunity retained at release.

Comprehensive closed-loop experiments demonstrate that RGD substantially
reduces slow-path invocations while preserving task outcomes, and that release
validation retains the corrective intervention in the evaluated proposal stream
while filtering harmful or negligible alternatives. The common action interface
was consistently evaluated across the considered slow reasoners, traffic
settings, and additional scenario families. By reconceiving
slow reasoning as an expiring proposal rather than a persistent command source,
RGD establishes a principled, release-aware decision interface between
deliberative reasoning and real-time control.
